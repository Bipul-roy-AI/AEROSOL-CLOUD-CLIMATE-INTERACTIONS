# Decades of transformation: quantifying urbanisation’s effects on the hydroclimate

**Critical literature review | Search conducted 4 November 2026 UTC**

## Abstract

Urbanisation reorganises water and energy fluxes through changes in vegetation, soils, built materials, building geometry, aerosols, drainage and imported water. The strongest general physical expectation is that connected imperviousness and engineered drainage accelerate storm runoff, while urban surfaces often warm relative to their surroundings. Their magnitudes, and even the sign of changes in evapotranspiration (ET), humidity, recharge and rainfall, depend on what the city replaced and how it is supplied with water. Multi-decadal records suggest that urban development can modify precipitation and hydrological trends, but a spatial urban–rural contrast is not, by itself, an urbanisation-induced temporal change. Recent global studies using the same IMERG satellite precipitation product find predominantly positive annual urban–rural rainfall contrasts, alongside exceptions, differences by intensity and sensitivity to the precipitation product. Evidence for a global urban effect on extreme precipitation remains contested because the outcome, counterfactual and scales differ. Coupled modelling, catchment observations and explicit comparison designs provide complementary but imperfect causal evidence. No effect size in this review is intended as a pooled global estimate.

## 1. Scope, definitions and search

This is a **selective critical narrative review**, not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Searches on 4 November 2026 covered OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar, web search, publisher and full-text pages, PubMed/PMC, government research records and citation trails; queries combined urbanisation with precipitation, temperature, heat islands, humidity, ET, runoff, flooding, groundwater and attribution. Foundational papers and research published up to the actual search date were eligible; the remainder of November 2026 cannot be searched prospectively. Sources were selected for conceptual importance, empirical design, contrasting findings, scale and accessible bibliographic information, not through an exhaustive screened corpus. Search results and accessible abstracts/full text are uneven; languages other than English, paywalled full texts, small cities and local monitoring reports are underrepresented. The alphaXiv CLI search was blocked by lack of authentication; some publisher/government endpoints returned access errors. Individual references below link to a publisher, DOI or official publication page; bibliographic checks do not imply independent reproduction of analyses. Supporting evidence and an audit trail accompany this review.

**Estimands matter.** Urbanisation is change in land use, infrastructure and human water/energy inputs, not simply urban population or the presence of a city. A *spatial urban–rural difference* at one time need not equal the effect of conversion over time. *Local* refers to within-city land, air and water; *downwind* to an advected meteorological footprint; *catchment* to a contributing drainage area; and *regional* to effects across multiple cities, basins or atmospheric grid cells. Downwind and hydrological downstream directions need not coincide. In an attribution experiment, the urban effect is the contrast between outcomes with and without a specified urban intervention, under specified weather, background climate and management. That counterfactual is not directly observed.

## 3. How a city changes the coupled system

A simplified balance illustrates why single-variable explanations fail. At an appropriately defined catchment and interval, precipitation plus imported water is partitioned among ET, discharge, exports and change in surface and subsurface storage. The energy balance partitions available radiative and anthropogenic energy among sensible and latent heat, storage and transport. Boundaries matter: a piped import or sewer export is an external flux for one basin but not necessarily for a larger one. Replacing vegetation and permeable soil with buildings and pavement changes both balances, but development also introduces vegetation management, irrigation, leaks and engineered subsurface pathways [5,6,8].

Reduced latent cooling can warm the urban surface and near-surface air; rough buildings redirect flow, change turbulent mixing and can strengthen or displace convergence. Urban heat, roughness and aerosol emissions may alter cloud development, while a reduction in local ET may counteract convective enhancement by limiting moisture [2,5,9]. Storms also import moisture from far beyond a city, so local ET is not the sole rainfall source. Subsequent rain encounters altered infiltration and drainage, feeding back on soil moisture, ET and heat. The sign of the complete feedback cannot be deduced from any one link. These pathways are physically plausible; the observed co-occurrence of heat and rainfall anomalies does not independently identify their causal contributions.

## 4. Temperature and urban heat islands over decades

Oke’s foundational city-size study helped establish the urban heat island as a measurable city-scale climate phenomenon [10]. Built materials store heat, canopy geometry changes radiation and ventilation, vegetation and water modify latent cooling, and anthropogenic heat can add energy. Effects depend strongly on time of day, season, cloud, wind, morphology and the rural comparator. Not every urban surface is warmer than every rural surface at every hour.

Du et al. [4] show why the distinction between **surface** and **canopy-air** heat islands matters: the global daytime trends in their 2003–2020 city sample are approximately 0.19 and 0.03 °C/decade, respectively (table). Their reported standard errors quantify precision of a sample mean, not all structural uncertainty. The observed contrast can change because the city warms, the rural reference changes, or both; it should not be added mechanically to a global warming trend. Satellite surface temperature samples skin temperature under retrieval conditions, often clear-sky, whereas residents experience shaded air temperatures and radiant conditions; satellite overpass timing is not a full diurnal cycle. Comparisons of present urban cores with present rural land also miss historic land-cover trajectories and rural agricultural or irrigation changes.

Climate-change warming and urbanisation can coexist and interact: absolute city temperature may rise under a changing background even with an unchanged urban–rural heat-island intensity. Conversely, increasing contrast is not necessarily a contribution to the global mean temperature. Heat exposure further depends on humidity, nighttime recovery, housing and population, none of which follows directly from a surface-temperature contrast.

## 5. Precipitation: local, downwind and regional effects

The METROMEX-era work identified urban rainfall anomalies as a research problem rather than a universally reproducible rainfall multiplier [11]. More recent satellite studies widen the sample beyond a few intensive field campaigns. Sui et al. [1] report positive annual urban–rural precipitation contrasts for 63% of 1,056 cities in 2001–2020; the authors also report downwind wet anomalies and strengthening of the urban wet-island signal over the study interval. A city–rural anomaly is not the same as total rainfall, and fixed city definitions complicate interpreting changes as the consequence of expansion. The dataset and selection of eligible large cities cannot support claims about every settlement.

Xiong et al. [2] find that the balance depends on rainfall intensity and time of day: more frequent or greater light-rain amounts coexist with reduced higher-intensity amounts in many city comparisons. The 63% positive total-amount fraction is not a 63% rainfall increase, and only a minority of all cities show statistically significant positive contrasts. Urban or rural gauges used for validation were geographically concentrated; satellite products can smooth small, intense convective cells and underestimate heavy rain. A 0.1° grid cannot resolve street-scale rainfall or a complete urban canopy. Both Sui and Xiong use IMERG, so their matching 63% shares are not independent product replications. Xiong et al. report that an alternative satellite product, GSMaP, did not effectively reproduce the expected global urban rainfall island, although IMERG performed better against the available gauges. Retrieval, gauge representativeness, coast, mountains and rural reference choice can all affect the inferred contrast.

Guccione et al. [3] provide a different long-term result: selected global gauges show stronger increases in the occurrence of locally defined extreme daily events at urban than at rural stations over 1962–2021. Their complementary 1981–2022 European simulation produces the distinct 17.8% versus 6.5% comparison in the table. Stations are classified using 2015 settlement conditions rather than their individual conversion histories; the authors also report a 1975-classification sensitivity check. Urban stations are not spatially random, so regional climate gradients, station history and baseline characteristics can generate part of the contrast. The source’s threshold counts events, rather than expressing a percentage change in daily rainfall depth. This analysis does not directly contradict Xiong et al.: daily station extremes, hourly satellite intensity classes, time-varying frequency and long-term spatial climatologies answer different questions.

The literature nevertheless disagrees on where and when extreme rain intensifies. Liu and Niyogi’s 2019 meta-analysis [9] pooled 85 study estimates from 48 papers and reported mean rainfall enhancements of 18% downwind and 16% over cities, with enhancement often 20–50 km from city centres. These are that review’s selected-literature estimates, not a new pooled estimate or an expected response for any individual city; study dependence, heterogeneous definitions, publication and city-selection biases constrain extrapolation. Yang et al. [12] analyse 1,790 inland cities during 2003–2018 and use convection-permitting simulations to investigate development pattern. Their city selection deliberately excludes complex terrain and land–water boundaries, and associations between compactness and storm properties do not by themselves identify a planning intervention’s effect. Prevailing winds, storm organisation, sea breezes and mountains can move maxima downwind, split or displace storms, or reverse the sign. Thus ‘urban rainfall’ should specify an urban footprint, downwind sector, event definition and reference period.

## 6. Evapotranspiration and atmospheric humidity

ET combines evaporation from soil and wet built surfaces with plant transpiration. Replacing a well-watered forest or paddy landscape with buildings can lower ET and increase sensible heating, particularly during the growing season [5,6]. Conversely, irrigation can plausibly make an arid urban landscape evaporate more water than its dry surroundings; the frequency and magnitude of such reversals need direct measurement. The relevant counterfactual is the vegetation, soil and water management actually displaced, not an abstract natural surface. Short wetting pulses on impervious surfaces, groundwater access of trees and water import complicate annual averages and remote-sensing retrievals.

Hao et al. [5] combine flux-site observations, remote-sensing ET, reanalysis and Chinese station records. The 215 mm/year mean urban deficit in the table pertains to 20 selected flux-site locations; it is not a global-area or population-weighted effect. Meteorological pairing partly uses surrounding ERA5 grid cells rather than matched independent rural towers. The separate Chinese analysis draws on 1980–2018 station histories in five agglomerations. Correlations between ET and humidity contrasts are consistent with coupling but cannot by themselves rule out shared climate or site-selection drivers.

Humidity must be specified. **Specific humidity** measures the mass of water vapour in air; **relative humidity** compares vapour pressure with saturation at the current temperature; **vapour-pressure deficit** measures the difference. Warming can lower relative humidity or increase vapour-pressure deficit without decreasing absolute water vapour. Both urban dry and moist islands occur, with climate, season, irrigation and water bodies influencing the sign [5,13]. Cooling by irrigation may reduce sensible heat but consume water and sometimes increase absolute humidity; its effect on human heat stress requires an appropriate thermal-stress metric, not temperature alone.

## 7. Runoff, flooding, infiltration and groundwater

At the parcel scale, pavement generally reduces direct infiltration through the covered soil. At the catchment scale, **hydrologically connected** pavement and drains can deliver water rapidly to channels, increasing runoff volume or peak flow and shortening response times for a given storm. Yet roofs may drain to permeable ground, disconnected green infrastructure may retain water, and detention, channelisation, culverts, antecedent moisture and storm spatial structure can change peaks in either direction. For very large storms, initially permeable soils may become saturated and infrastructure can fail or overflow. A rise in discharge is therefore not a fixed function of total impervious percentage [6,14].

Urban flood hazard is not identical to rainfall, discharge or damages. Flooding may be pluvial, riverine, coastal or compound; rainfall timing relative to tides, river stage and drain capacity matters. Urbanisation also puts more people and assets in exposed locations. Mazzoleni et al. [7] link urban area and rainfall statistically to a Landsat-derived annual maximum surface-water/flood-extent proxy in large basins, but their 57% figure describes selection of explanatory variables, not a causal flood increment. The observation-dependent proxy is not a complete event inventory: short-lived urban flash floods may be missed because of revisit timing, cloud cover and water classification. In Houston, Zhang et al. [15] use coupled modelling to investigate how urban land cover affected Hurricane Harvey rainfall and flood response: this is a physically informative event counterfactual, not an estimate applicable to every hurricane or a clean estimate of the separate effects of global warming. Model configuration and storm evolution constrain generalisation.

**Infiltration is not aquifer recharge.** Water infiltrating near a street can be taken up by vegetation, enter shallow drains or reach groundwater; recharge may be displaced from the paved parcel to a nearby depression. In Austin, the water-balance example [6] estimates declining direct natural recharge but higher total recharge when leakage and irrigation return are added (table). This is a historical estimate reported in a review of a case study, not evidence that all urban aquifers gain water. Water and sewer leaks may introduce contaminants. Pumping, underground construction and drainage can lower groundwater levels even if gross recharge rises; conversely falling abstraction can raise levels. Water levels alone therefore cannot identify recharge. Recharge estimates need tracer or isotope information, leakage inventories, water budgets, aquifer properties and pumping histories [8].

## 8. Methods and causal separation

| Method | What it can establish | Main limitations and useful checks |
|---|---|---|
| Long station, gauge and streamflow histories | Local trends, event timing and, with co-observations, evolving water/energy balances. | Station relocation, instrument/siting changes, gaps, rating curves and concurrent drainage/land use can mimic trends. Homogenise; preserve metadata and examine rain–runoff events and uncertainty. |
| Urban–rural or upwind–downwind contrasts | Spatial footprint under broadly shared weather; scalable across many cities. | Rural baselines may have different elevation, coast, irrigation, vegetation or be affected by urban plumes. Vary controls, radius and wind sectors; report both signs and significance. |
| Remote sensing | Consistent coverage of land cover, surface temperature, rainfall proxies and floodwater over multiple regions. | Pixel mixing, short records, clear-sky selection, sensor transitions, coarse rainfall resolution and flood revisit gaps. Validate against independent gauges/towers; retain retrieval uncertainty and observation scale. |
| Matched catchments, before–after and panel/difference-in-differences | Changes relative to control areas and shared temporal variability. | Requires credible matching, stable observations and parallel pre-trends; development may be endogenous, controls may urbanise and treatment spills across boundaries. Test pre-trends and sensitivity; avoid conditioning on mediators if estimating the total effect. |
| Counterfactual coupled atmospheric–hydrological modelling | Mechanistic experiments separating urban land cover, emissions or drainage, conditional on specified settings. | Land-cover replacement, boundary conditions, urban physics, aerosols and initial-condition chaos matter. Use ensembles and multiple plausible counterfactuals; evaluate rainfall, fluxes and discharge independently. |
| Climate/event attribution ensembles | Contrast specified worlds with and without anthropogenic climate forcing, sometimes combined with land-cover experiments. | Different forcing and intervention definitions; interactions mean effects need not add linearly. Report conditional probability or intensity and uncertainty, not a single unqualified causal fraction. |

A useful factorial design holds atmospheric forcing fixed while varying historical versus observed urban land cover, and separately varies climatic forcing. Its interaction term tests whether the combined response differs from the sum of the separate responses. Repeat across weather years and initial-condition ensembles; compare against a rural or less-urbanised control with compatible elevation, seasonality and water management. A single historical/non-urban simulation cannot identify a universal climate-versus-urban contribution. In observational analysis, accounting for natural variability requires long baseline records, relevant circulation and storm covariates, interannual dependence, and uncertainty clustered at appropriate spatial and temporal scales. Aerosols, irrigation and engineering may be part of the **urban treatment**, not merely nuisances: whether to adjust for them depends on whether the desired estimand is the total effect or a controlled pathway-specific effect.

## 9. Disagreements, geographical bias and priorities

1. **Rainfall extremes:** apparent discrepancies between negative hourly heavy-rain contrasts and positive urban–rural differences in trends of daily extremes may be substantive or methodological; matched outcome definitions and independent observations are needed [2,3]. Downwind effects can contaminate rural controls.
2. **ET, humidity and recharge:** direction depends on prior land cover, climate and imported-water management [5,6,8]. Arid irrigated cities deserve particular attention, as do groundwater quality and delayed effects.
3. **Representativeness:** global city satellite samples still preferentially capture sufficiently large cities and relatively recent decades; long and reliable gauges and validation towers are unequally distributed. The cited studies do not establish globally balanced representation of smaller cities or informal settlements, and the urban flux-site sample is especially selective. Geographic scope of a gridded dataset is not the same as geographically balanced validation; a region-by-region inventory of direct observations remains an open need.
4. **Confounding:** greenhouse forcing, decadal variability, land-use conversion outside the city, air pollution, water imports, river regulation and infrastructure change jointly over time. Assigning an observed trend wholly to urbanisation or wholly to climate change is usually unjustified.
5. **High-value next studies:** establish paired urban–rural flux and humidity observations with leakage and irrigation inventories; reconstruct time-varying urban form and station metadata; combine event-scale radar–gauge rainfall with discharge and groundwater tracers; evaluate factorial urban/climate ensembles across humid, arid, coastal and mountainous settings. Pre-register estimands and controls, report negative and null results, and publish data, code and uncertainty ranges. Such designs would test competing mechanisms rather than extrapolate a universal multiplier.

## 10. Conclusions

Urbanisation is a coupled hydroclimatic intervention, not merely an increment in temperature or an impervious-area percentage. The most defensible synthesis is conditional: local surface warming and rapid runoff are frequent; ET, humidity and aquifer recharge depend on the water and land-use counterfactual; rainfall responses can be local or displaced downwind and differ by intensity, storm regime and climate. Multi-decadal attribution remains materially harder than mapping spatial contrasts. Progress requires combining long observations, explicit counterfactual designs and coupled models, with much stronger coverage of under-measured regions and infrastructure.
